# Crash insurance and the G10 carry premium

Amanjeet Singh. Extended abstract of the draft of 26 September 2026; the paper is [paper.pdf](paper.pdf) (source [paper.tex](paper.tex)).

This paper asks whether the price of crash insurance quoted in G10 currency option smiles accounts for the carry premium, and for its change between the zero-rate regime (May 2013 to December 2021) and the hiking regime that began in January 2022. I fixed the design and its interpretation rules on 23 September 2026, before computing any estimate.

I obtained the data from LSEG Workspace under a student licence provided by my university, which covers only me and does not permit redistribution, so only aggregate results are published and anyone rerunning the work needs their own LSEG Workspace licence. Composite dealer quotes for nine currencies against the dollar are sampled at New York month-ends from May 2013 to August 2026, giving 160 month-ends and 159 return months. Fenics quotes from January 2007 (72 usable month-ends) serve as secondary evidence, and VIX futures settlements come from Cboe.

SABR smiles with β = 1 (Hagan et al., 2002) are calibrated to at-the-money, 25Δ risk-reversal and 25Δ market-strangle quotes (Reiswich and Wystup, 2012). Each leg of a three-long, three-short portfolio sorted on the forward discount (Lustig, Roussanov and Verdelhan, 2011) is protected by a one-month 10Δ option, following the hedged construction of Burnside et al. (2011). The primary estimand, φ, is the skew premium of that protection per unit of forward discount, compared across the two regimes. The other estimands ask whether φ predicts carry returns, split the realised hedge cost into payoff, volatility-level and skew terms, repeat the analysis under the other reading of the butterfly quote, and measure exposure to a VIX roll-down (Caballero and Doyle, 2012) and to global FX volatility (Menkhoff et al., 2012).

Under the pre-registered rules the answer is partial support only. The point estimate of φ falls from 0.809 to 0.454, but the difference of −0.355 has a Newey–West standard error of 0.204 and a bootstrap interval of [−0.814, 0.014], so I do not claim it. A supplementary split, added after the first results, shows that the skew cost of protection hardly moved (11.24 and 11.19 bp per month) while the forward-discount spread rose by 8.44 bp; realised carry returns were also higher after 2022 (37.0 against 7.4 bp per month), though not significantly so. φ predicts carry returns in sample (bias-corrected one-sided p = 0.021) but not out of sample, where the Clark–West statistic of 1.639 does not reach the 5% critical value of 1.645. The mean realised hedge cost is close to the skew premium paid ex ante (−11.2 bp per month). The hedge-cost ratio θ_UB is 0.53 against a diffusive null of about 0.10, but its confidence set is unbounded because the unhedged mean (17.7 bp, t = 1.47) is itself insignificant, and I do not read it as a crash share. Unhedged carry loads on the VIX roll-down (0.049, t = 7.41), and the hedge lowers that loading by about a fifth. Only a ten-currency ranking makes the fall in φ significant under both inferences, and there too it comes from the forward-discount spread. The evidence points to a wider carry spread rather than a change in the price of crash insurance.

The paper adds a decomposition of the realised cost of G10 crash insurance over 2013–2026, with a regime comparison of its ex-ante skew component, to the option-hedged carry evidence of Jurek (2014), together with a pre-registered test of whether the skew price explains the change in the carry premium. The two readings of the butterfly quote, which Bossens et al. (2010) show can differ markedly for skewed smiles, move φ by under 4 per cent here. On the theory side, the currency-premium decomposition of Backus, Foresi and Telmer (2001), in the entropy form of Backus, Chernov and Zin (2014), is restated with the covered-interest-parity basis as a wedge, as Lustig and Verdelhan (2019) do with a general wedge. My own additions are bounds on option-implied moments from truncated quotes under a stated tail condition, which extend the truncation bounds of Jiang and Tian (2005), and an exact form with an error bound for the diffusive null of the hedge cost, whose leading-order form is due to Farhi et al. (2015).

The hiking regime is short, with roughly 8 effective observations per regime in a post hoc calculation, and the cross-section has nine currencies. θ_UB is weakly identified, the realised terms depend on the crashes that happened to occur, and the moment intervals rest on tail assumptions. The quotes are composite, payoffs use end-of-day spot rather than the 10:00 New York cut, dates follow New York holidays only, the description of the three-month USD OIS series is unconfirmed, quote revisions are unchecked and the out-of-sample result depends on the training start. Requiring both intervals to exclude zero before a regime difference counts is my reading of the E1 rule.
